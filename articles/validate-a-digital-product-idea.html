How to validate a digital product idea before building it

A low-cost process for finding out whether a real problem exists before you spend weeks making a solution.

NEXORA EDITORIAL · PRODUCT THINKING

A digital product can be beautifully designed and still fail to solve a problem people care enough to address. Before building a spreadsheet, template, course, or small app, test the problem itself. The goal is not to collect compliments. It is to find evidence that a specific group already spends time, effort, or money dealing with the issue.

Start with a narrow problem

“People need to be more productive” is too broad to guide a product. A more useful statement names a group, a situation, and a recurring obstacle: “Independent designers lose track of which client invoices are still unpaid.” You can investigate that situation and check whether your proposed solution actually helps.

Write down three things before doing research: who experiences the problem, when it happens, and what they currently do about it. If you cannot describe the current workaround, you probably do not understand the problem yet.

Talk about past behavior, not hypothetical interest

Ask a few people who fit the audience about the last time the problem happened. Useful questions include:

  • “Can you walk me through the last time this happened?”
  • “What did you do to handle it?”
  • “What was frustrating or time-consuming?”
  • “Have you tried another tool or paid for a solution?”

Avoid leading questions such as “Would you buy my app?” People are often encouraging when a product is only an idea. Specific stories about recent behavior are more informative than guesses about future purchases.

Look for evidence, not applause

Pay attention to repeated patterns: several people describing the same obstacle, existing workarounds, spreadsheets or notes they maintain, and tools they already pay for. One enthusiastic response is not proof of demand. A repeated problem across people in the same audience is a stronger reason to investigate further.

Test the smallest useful version

Do not build the entire product to test the core promise. Create the smallest demonstration that lets someone experience the main benefit. For a tracker, that might be a sample spreadsheet with a few realistic example rows. For a guide, it might be one complete, useful section. For a workflow tool, it might be a clickable mock-up or a short walkthrough.

Make the limitations clear. A prototype should not pretend to be a finished service, and example data should never be presented as real customer results.

Use a simple validation sequence

  1. Define: write one specific problem statement and identify the intended audience.
  2. Research: speak with people in that audience about recent experiences and current workarounds.
  3. Compare: review existing alternatives and note what they do well, what they cost, and what users still struggle with.
  4. Prototype: make one small example that demonstrates the central benefit.
  5. Observe: ask someone to try it and watch where they hesitate or get confused.
  6. Decide: improve the idea, narrow the audience, or stop if the evidence is weak.

Know when to change direction

If people describe the problem but ignore the prototype, the solution may be wrong or too complicated. If they like the concept but cannot recall experiencing the problem, the audience may be too broad or the problem too weak. If a simple existing tool already solves it well, a new product needs a clear reason to exist.

Stopping is useful information. It is cheaper to change direction after a few conversations than after weeks of building.

Keep the experiment honest

Do not invent testimonials, fake customer counts, or imply that a waiting list proves people will pay. If you publish a test page, explain what is available and what is only a concept. Collect as little personal information as possible, and do not take payment for something you cannot deliver clearly and responsibly.

Nexora note: validation reduces uncertainty; it does not guarantee commercial success. This guide is educational and is not financial or legal advice.
